CUES - Graduate thesis, mentor Dr. Erogbogbo, two UCSF pediatric surgeons - Stoma: 1.5 million Americans; prolapse in about 1 in 4 - Even with surgery, about 50% chance it comes back - 1 how it works, 2 first preliminary devices, 3 two-piece prototypes, 4 animal study - Team of 8; over $100,000 in grants including a Phase 1 STTR SAY IT For my graduate thesis, I worked on a device called the NoLapse. Under my mentor Dr. Erogbogbo, I worked with two UCSF pediatric surgeons to take their preliminary device and begin the process of turning it into an approved medical device. Some background first. A stoma is a surgically created outlet to bypass intestinal conditions. It affects about 1.5 million Americans. Already a stoma is not ideal, but on top of this, a stoma prolapse can happen, which can be extremely uncomfortable or even require more surgery. This happens to about 1 in 4 colostomy patients. And even with surgery, there's still about a 50 percent chance it happens again. So there's a clear need for a non-surgical alternative. Picture one shows how it works. It goes in through the opening, and the lower ring opens up underneath and holds everything in place. Picture two is where we started, the first preliminary devices. Picture three is our two-piece prototypes, in multiple sizes for a variety of patients. And picture four is the device in place during our animal study. I coordinated a team of eight students. And I accumulated over a hundred thousand dollars in grants, including a Phase 1 STTR. (~50 seconds)
